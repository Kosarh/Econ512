01 · Motivation

Why price the floodplain at all?

Flooding is among the most common and costly natural hazards in the United States. It generates billions of dollars in damages and large fiscal and housing-market externalities.

For decades, subsidized or weakly risk-based National Flood Insurance Program premiums lowered the user cost of high-risk housing and encouraged development on exposed ground. Taxpayers and the government bore that mispricing.

FEMA’s Risk Rating 2.0 aligns premiums with property-level flood risk. Raising the cost of flood-prone housing is a persistent user-cost shock. It can move prices and stocks inside the floodplain and in nearby safer markets.

Hedonic studies capitalize flood risk into prices, but they hold the housing stock fixed, rarely model spillovers to safer markets, and do not separate short-run capitalization from long-run quantity adjustment.

02 · The damage

What mispriced insurance left in the water.

Destroyed housing is the fiscal and human cost that weakly risk-based premiums helped concentrate where the water returns.

04 · Mechanism · the current arrives here

One shock. Two banks.

Inside the floodplain

  1. User cost rises: ψFP ↑
  2. Willingness to pay falls
  3. Price jumps down on impact: PFP ↓
  4. Construction and redevelopment slow
  5. Long run: the stock SFP contracts

Outside the floodplain

  1. Demand shifts toward safer housing
  2. Prices come under pressure: PNF ↑
  3. Construction responds
  4. Long run: the stock SNF expands
  5. The outside move is only indirect
Safer-market expansion only partly offsets the floodplain contraction, so total regional stock falls. No arbitrage. Free mobility. Perfect substitution between floodplain and non-floodplain housing in the baseline. Because ψNF is unchanged, the outside market does not fully replace what leaves the floodplain.

06 · Evidence · Allegheny County, 82% premium increase

The floodplain takes the loss. Safer ground barely moves.

On impact, stocks are fixed. Floodplain prices fall 28.871%, and safer prices rise 0.054%. In the long run the floodplain stock falls 33.456%, the safer stock rises 0.180%, and total regional stock falls 0.175%. The safer market is 98.95% of the initial stock, so the spillover is positive and small — and it does not replace the housing that leaves.

07 · Where the river ends

Safer ground, tighter markets.

Risk Rating 2.0 can reduce development in high-risk areas and support climate adaptation.

It can also tighten regional supply and worsen affordability when destination markets cannot expand quickly or elastically enough.

Complementary housing-supply policies may be needed to reduce those affordability costs.

Housing is the principal store of non-wage wealth for many low- and middle-income households. Capitalization losses and higher housing costs may bring financial hardship, forced doubling-up, or housing instability. Those distributional consequences motivate a separate welfare analysis and are not yet quantified.

These simulations are disciplined equilibrium counterfactuals, not causal estimates of Risk Rating 2.0.
